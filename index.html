Help & Guide

Learn what each chart indicator means and how to use the platform's features effectively.

EMA (20) — Exponential Moving Average

Full Form: Exponential Moving Average (20-period)

What it does: The EMA smooths out price data by calculating a weighted average of the last 20 candles (time periods). Unlike a simple average, it gives more weight to recent prices, making it react faster to new trends.

How to read it:

  • 📈 Price above EMA line → Stock is in an uptrend (bullish momentum)
  • 📉 Price below EMA line → Stock is in a downtrend (bearish momentum)
  • 🔄 Price crosses EMA from below → Potential buy signal
  • 🔄 Price crosses EMA from above → Potential sell signal

💡 Tip: The EMA-20 works best for short-to-medium term swing trading. Combine it with RSI for stronger confirmation signals.

RSI — Relative Strength Index

Full Form: Relative Strength Index (14-period)

What it does: RSI measures the speed and magnitude of recent price changes to evaluate whether a stock is overbought or oversold. It oscillates between 0 and 100.

How to read it:

  • 🔴 RSI > 70 → Stock is overbought — price may drop soon (sell signal)
  • 🟢 RSI < 30 → Stock is oversold — price may rebound (buy signal)
  • ⚖️ RSI 40–60 → Neutral zone — no strong signal

💡 Tip: RSI below 30 is often the best time to buy undervalued stocks. Combine it with EMA crossovers for even stronger confirmation.

MACD — Moving Average Convergence Divergence

Full Form: Moving Average Convergence Divergence

What it does: MACD tracks the relationship between two EMAs (usually 12-period and 26-period). It shows momentum shifts and trend direction changes — essentially telling you when a trend is gaining or losing steam.

Components:

  • 📊 MACD Line = EMA(12) − EMA(26) — shows momentum direction
  • 📈 Signal Line = EMA(9) of the MACD — acts as a trigger
  • 📉 Histogram = MACD − Signal — shows the gap between them

How to read it:

  • 🟢 MACD crosses above Signal → Bullish crossover (buy signal)
  • 🔴 MACD crosses below Signal → Bearish crossover (sell signal)
  • 📊 Histogram growing → Trend is strengthening
  • 📊 Histogram shrinking → Trend is weakening

💡 Tip: MACD is best for identifying trend reversals. Use it alongside EMA and RSI — when all three align (e.g., price crosses above EMA + RSI rising from oversold + MACD bullish crossover), it's a very strong buy signal.

Smart Trade Advisor — How It Works & Key Metrics
🎯 1. What is the Smart Trade Advisor?

The Smart Trade Advisor is ToroFolio's real-time valuation engine. It combines fundamental value metrics (Graham Intrinsic Value) and technical momentum indicators (Pivot Support & Resistance) to calculate whether a stock is a Strong Buy, Accumulate, or Sell / Avoid.

📈 2. Speedometer Rating Gauge (10% - 90%)

The visual gauge rates the asset's overall health score based on 4 core pillars:

  • P/E vs Industry P/E: Stocks trading below industry P/E receive score boosts.
  • Graham Intrinsic Discount: Prices below Graham Value add up to +15% score.
  • Return on Equity (ROE): Higher ROE (>15%) increases confidence rating.
  • Debt to Equity Ratio: Low or zero debt companies receive safety bonuses.
💎 3. Benjamin Graham Intrinsic Value

Calculated as √(22.5 × EPS × Book Value). Named after the father of value investing, Benjamin Graham. If current market price is below this intrinsic value, the stock is trading at an attractive Discount.

🎯 4. Perfect Entry (Pivot Support S1)

Calculated dynamically from today's High, Low, and Close price action. Represents the primary technical support floor where institutional buying interest creates an optimal low-risk entry price.

🚀 5. Target Profit (Pivot Resistance R1)

Represents the upper technical resistance ceiling. This is the optimal price zone to consider locking in profits or executing limit exit orders.

💡 Pro Tip: For best results, use the Smart Trade Advisor alongside Google Gemini AI in the AI Planner tab for personalized multi-timeframe analysis!

Fundamentals of Stocks — Key Definitions
1. Market Cap (Market Capitalization)

Market cap is the total market value of all of a company's outstanding shares. It represents the public's evaluation of the company's total equity value.

2. P/E Ratio (Price-to-Earnings Ratio)

P/E (price-to-earnings) ratio is the ratio of a company's share price to its earnings per share (EPS). P/E ratio is used to determine whether a stock is overvalued or undervalued relative to its earnings power.

3. P/B Ratio (Price-to-Book Ratio)

P/B (price-to-book) ratio is the ratio of a company's share price to its book value. Any value under 1.0 is generally considered a good valuation buffer, representing that the stock trades below its asset liquidating value.

4. Industry P/E

The average P/E ratio of all the stocks in any sector. Different sectors consider different P/E ratios as healthy depending on growth potential, regulatory overhead, and margins.

5. Debt to Equity

Debt to Equity is the percentage of the total liabilities of a company (debt) to its shareholders' equity. A higher debt to equity means the company is using more debt funds than equity funds, and a lower debt to equity means more equity than debt funds.

6. ROE (Return on Equity)

Return on equity (ROE) is a measure of financial performance, calculated by dividing net income by shareholders' equity. It reflects how efficiently a firm manages the capital that shareholders have invested.

7. Book Value

The amount of money that a company's shareholders would earn if the company is liquidated and has paid off all liabilities. It is the net asset value of the company.

8. Dividend Yield

Dividend yield percentage is the amount of money a company pays its shareholders for owning a share of its stock divided by its current stock price. A high dividend yield indicates strong cash distribution relative to the share price.

9. EPS (Earnings Per Share)

Earnings per share (EPS) is a company's net profit divided by the number of its common outstanding shares. It indicates how much money a company makes for each share of its stock.

10. Face Value

The original value of a company's stock as written in its books of accounts and its share certificates. Also known as par value, it is fixed when the stock is first issued and is used to calculate dividend payouts.
